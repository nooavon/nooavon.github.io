## Question

Patents encourage innovation by granting a temporary monopoly. In pharmaceuticals, development is very expensive (median about $1.1 billion per new drug), but monopoly pricing can keep life-saving treatment from people who cannot pay. Is there a better balance between rewarding innovation and getting medicines to people?

## Approach

- Sets out the trade-off between the rate of innovation and the social benefit of each invention, using the innovation-rent framework from CORE's *The Economy*.
- Reviews evidence on drug development costs, first-mover advantage without patents, and the welfare effects of introducing drug patents in India.
- Compares two alternatives to today's system: weaker, more selective patents, and prize-based rewards for medical breakthroughs.

## Main arguments

- **Patents are not the only incentive.** Even without them, an innovator in pharmaceuticals keeps a lead of around four years (Lanjouw, 1998), against the 20 years a patent gives.
- **Today's patents are probably too strong.** Patent policy is shaped mostly by those who benefit from it (Boldrin and Levine, 2013). Giving up a little innovation for much wider access could leave society better off.
- **Prizes could beat monopolies.** In India, patents on one drug family caused an estimated $300 million welfare loss against a $20 million private gain (Chaudhuri, Goldberg and Jia, 2006). Paying innovators any amount in between could leave both sides better off, as proposed by Kremer and Williams (2009).
- **A mix may work best.** Treatments judged essential could be bought out through prizes and made public, while patents remain for less critical innovations.
